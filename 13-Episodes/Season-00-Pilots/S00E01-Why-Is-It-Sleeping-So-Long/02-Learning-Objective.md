# S00E01 --- Why Is It Sleeping So Long?

## 02 --- Learning Objective

## Primary Learning Objective

After the episode, the audience should understand:

> **A plant that looks brown or slow to wake up after winter is not
> necessarily completely dead. Look for evidence before deciding what
> happened or pruning/removing it.**

The episode teaches observation before diagnosis.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Scientific Focus

The episode will use **winter injury / winter drying (desiccation)** as
the main scientific context.

Winter desiccation occurs when plant tissues lose water while frozen
soil prevents roots from replacing it efficiently. Wind, sun, dry fall
conditions and frozen soil can increase this stress.

In Alberta, winter desiccation is a recognized form of winter injury,
and browned or "burned" evergreen foliage is common.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Dormancy Is Different

Dormancy is a normal seasonal process.

Woody plants acclimate and enter dormancy as seasons change. Timing of
spring bud break varies by species and environmental conditions.

Therefore:

**late bud break alone is not proof of winter injury.**

The episode must not teach that every plant that wakes later than
neighboring plants is damaged.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Winter Injury Is an Umbrella Term

"Winter injury" may result from several mechanisms, including:

-   desiccation / winter drying;
-   extreme cold;
-   rapid temperature changes;
-   frost injury;
-   snow or ice damage;
-   root injury;
-   sunscald;
-   other environmental stresses.

The episode should not imply that every brown branch was caused
specifically by desiccation unless the visual evidence and story
establish that interpretation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Recommended Pilot Plant

For the clearest visual teaching, the pilot should use a **generic hardy
evergreen shrub** rather than locking a precise species at this stage.

Why:

-   winter drying is visually understandable on evergreen foliage;
-   brown/bleached exposed foliage contrasts clearly with living buds or
    protected tissue;
-   wind/sun exposure can be shown visually;
-   Alberta documentation specifically recognizes evergreen needle
    discoloration from desiccation.

A species may be canonically selected later if production requires
botanical specificity.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Observable Evidence

Sprout should work from evidence that can actually be seen.

Candidate clues:

### Brown or Bleached Foliage

Some exposed evergreen foliage is brown, tan, reddish or bleached.

### Uneven Damage

Damage may be stronger on exposed, sunny or windward portions.

### Protected Tissue Looks Better

More sheltered portions may retain healthier color or condition.

### Living Buds

Damaged foliage does not automatically mean every bud is dead.

Living buds can remain capable of producing new growth.

This provides the strongest candidate for Sprout's key discovery.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Sprout's Key Discovery

Sprout looks closely at the apparently damaged plant.

At first:

**brown = dead**

seems plausible.

Then she notices:

**a living bud.**

This changes the question.

The plant is no longer simply classified as "dead."

Some tissue may have suffered winter injury while viable tissue remains.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Discovery Frame Candidate

The Discovery Frame can enlarge the living bud.

The visual contrast should be simple:

**damaged foliage around it**

versus

**small viable bud**

The device should help the audience see exactly what changed Sprout's
interpretation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What Brooks Can Explain

After Sprout discovers the evidence, Brooks can explain in
age-appropriate language:

> Plants can lose water during winter, especially when sun and wind keep
> drying their exposed parts while the ground is frozen.

He may then connect:

> Some parts can be damaged even when the whole plant isn't dead.

The exact dialogue will be written later.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What Brooks Must NOT Claim

Brooks must not say:

-   brown foliage proves winter desiccation;
-   a green/living bud proves the entire plant is healthy;
-   all plants should wake at the same time;
-   winter damage is always caused by cold;
-   every damaged plant will fully recover;
-   all brown foliage will become green again;
-   the audience can diagnose every plant from appearance alone.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Important Scientific Distinction

For evergreen winter injury:

**dead brown foliage generally does not turn green again.**

However, surviving buds may produce new growth and help fill damaged
areas.

This distinction is useful because it prevents the story from suggesting
magical recovery of already-dead foliage.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Care Message

The care lesson is intentionally cautious:

> **Observe before you cut.**

Do not immediately remove or heavily prune a plant solely because it
looks bad at the end of winter.

For evergreen winter injury, waiting until spring growth clarifies which
buds and branches survived can provide better evidence before pruning.

The episode is educational, not a universal diagnostic or pruning
prescription.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Child-Level Mental Model

The scientific idea should reduce to:

**Winter can hurt part of a plant without killing all of it.**

And the behavioral lesson becomes:

**Look closer before deciding.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Vocabulary

### Primary Audience Vocabulary

Use naturally:

-   bud;
-   branch;
-   winter;
-   wind;
-   sun;
-   water;
-   dry;
-   alive;
-   damaged;

### Secondary / Optional Vocabulary

May be introduced carefully:

-   dormancy;

-   desiccation.

"Desiccation" should not be required for understanding the episode.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Evidence Logic

The story's reasoning should be:

``` text
The plant looks brown.
↓
Brown appearance raises a question.
↓
Appearance alone is not enough.
↓
Sprout looks closer.
↓
She finds viable tissue / a living bud.
↓
Therefore the whole plant cannot simply be assumed dead.
↓
Brooks explains how winter can damage exposed tissue.
↓
They wait for more spring evidence before making an irreversible decision.
```

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Educational Misconceptions to Avoid

### Misconception 1

"Brown plant = dead plant."

Correction:

Brown tissue may be dead or damaged, but the condition of the entire
plant requires more evidence.

### Misconception 2

"Winter injury = freezing."

Correction:

Winter injury has multiple causes, including drying.

### Misconception 3

"Spring arrives and every plant wakes together."

Correction:

Species and conditions influence dormancy and bud break.

### Misconception 4

"If one bud is alive, everything is fine."

Correction:

A living bud is evidence of viable tissue, not proof that the entire
plant is undamaged.

### Misconception 5

"Brown evergreen foliage will turn green again."

Correction:

Dead brown foliage generally does not green up; surviving buds may
produce new growth.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Scientific Accuracy Gate for Artwork

The images must support the explanation.

Do not generate arbitrary "plant damage" and then label it winter
desiccation.

The visual design should intentionally show:

-   evergreen shrub;
-   localized/exposed browning;
-   remaining viable bud/tissue;
-   plausible early-spring setting;
-   no obvious disease or animal damage that would create a competing
    explanation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why This Works for S00E01

This topic supports the Lawn With Care Universe educational method
because the answer cannot be obtained by simply looking from a distance.

Sprout must:

**LOOK CLOSER.**

Brooks must:

**WAIT FOR EVIDENCE.**

The scientific content therefore reinforces character canon rather than
interrupting the story.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Final Learning Statement

If the audience remembers only one idea:

> **Looking damaged after winter does not always mean the whole plant is
> dead. Observe the buds and new growth before deciding what to do.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------
