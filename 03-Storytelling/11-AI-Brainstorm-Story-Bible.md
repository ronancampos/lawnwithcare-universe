# Lawn With Care Universe
# Story Bible for AI Brainstorming

> "Great stories don't teach people what to think. They help people
> discover what they were ready to notice."

---

## What This Document Is

A **portable, self-contained** creative brief for brainstorming new
episode ideas for the Lawn With Care Universe with any AI tool
(ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc.), outside the production repository.
Paste this whole document into a fresh chat, then ask for episode
ideas, taglines, or dialogue drafts.

This is a **brainstorming aid, not a production pipeline.** It
deliberately excludes image-generation prompts, file paths, and
technical workflow — those live in a separate production repository
and don't matter for idea generation. Anything produced from this
document is a **draft proposal**, not officially canon, until the
project owner reviews it.

---

## The Pitch

An educational family franchise built on curiosity, observation, and
care for nature. Central creative principle:

> **The extraordinary already exists. We simply help people notice it.**

No magic. No fantasy elements. Nature is the true protagonist —
stories reveal real-world wonder rather than inventing anything to
make nature interesting. Every story keeps one promise:

> "By the end of this story, you will notice something in the real
> world that you probably never noticed before."

If a story idea can't keep that promise, it doesn't belong here.

---

## The World

**Yard Rangers** — an in-universe educational/stewardship
organization. Naturalists, mentors, observers, educators, caretakers
of curiosity. Explicitly **not** police, military, superheroes, or any
authority-through-power figure.

**Lawn With Care** — the real-world sponsor/brand behind the
franchise. It influences color palette, visual symbols, and the
philosophy of care, but the fiction is never an advertisement — the
brand supports the universe quietly through values, not promotion.

**Setting** — southern Alberta, Canada, in flavor (residential yards,
backyards, neighborhoods). Intentionally *recognizable, not
hyper-localized* — don't force geography into dialogue; a light touch
is enough.

## The Characters

### Ranger Brooks

Adult mentor and Senior Yard Ranger. Calm, patient, observant,
scientific, kind, humble, optimistic. His defining behavioral
principle: **observe before explaining.** He never lectures — he asks,
observes, compares, investigates, and celebrates. He is not the hero
because he knows more; he's the hero because he helps others discover
what they're capable of noticing.

Never: arrogant, cruel, impulsive, cynical, dismissive, aggressive,
authoritarian, sarcastic toward children.

Signature line: *"Let's look a little closer."*
Signature questions: *"What do you notice?" "What makes you say
that?" "What else could explain this?"*
He's allowed — encouraged — to say *"I don't know yet."*

### Sprout

Child protagonist (fixed at 9 years old — don't age her up or down,
don't use an age range). Curious, kind, gentle, hopeful, observant,
brave enough to ask questions. She's not smarter than other children —
she's simply more attentive. She should make the audience think *"that
could be me,"* never *"I could never be that smart."*

Never: mean, arrogant, reckless, cynical, competitive, afraid of
learning, embarrassed by curiosity.

Her curiosity usually starts the story. She owns the key discovery in
almost every episode.

### Brooks + Sprout

**Brooks guides. Sprout discovers.** And sometimes — Sprout shows
Brooks something he never noticed before. Their relationship is
mentor + learner, but also explorer + explorer. Brooks lowers himself
to her eye level for real conversations rather than talking down.
Neither replaces the other's role. Discovery ownership belongs to
Sprout; interpretation/context is Brooks' contribution, offered only
*after* she's found the evidence.

## The Storytelling DNA

**Emotional rhythm every story follows:**
Calm → Curiosity → Discovery → Investigation → Understanding →
Reflection → Hope.

**Stories begin small.** Never an explosion, never danger, never
chaos. A feather, a strange leaf, an unusual footprint, a puddle, a
mushroom — something almost everyone else would ignore. The first
question matters more than the first answer.

**No villains.** Nature provides enough mystery on its own. Questions
replace enemies; misunderstandings replace evil; investigation
replaces confrontation. Stakes are emotional (*will they understand
the mystery?*), never catastrophic.

**One discovery per episode.** Depth over quantity — one memorable
discovery beats ten forgotten facts.

**Discovery Frame** (a visual device, referenced here for narrative
purposes only): when it appears, pace slows and attention sharpens —
it's a storytelling tool, not decoration. Useful in brainstorming to
mark "this is the close-look moment" in a beat sheet.

**Endings never really end.** Stories close on *"what else might we
discover?"* not *"The End."* The audience should feel: *I want to go
outside. I want to look closer. I want to tell someone. I never
noticed that before.*

## The Little Prince Lens — Read This Before Brainstorming

This is the single most important craft lesson from this franchise's
production history, and the fastest way to tell a *good* Lawn With
Care idea from a merely *correct* one.

An early draft of two episodes taught real science accurately, with
warm dialogue — and still felt flat, like an explainer video with
characters attached. What fixed them wasn't more detail. It was
adding exactly what *Le Petit Prince* does: the essential is invisible
to the eye, and grown-ups are the ones who forgot how to look. A good
episode doesn't just report a fact through a child character — it
uses a small, ordinary observation to reveal something a careful heart
can see and a hurried eye cannot.

**Concretely, a strong episode has all four of these:**

1. **A small doorway, not a big event.** The mystery starts from
   something anyone could walk past without noticing — a brown patch,
   a droopy leaf, a worn path. Never a crisis.
2. **Personal stakes — the observer is implicated.** The best version
   of a mystery isn't just something Sprout studies from outside; at
   some point she recognizes she's *part of it.* (Example: in a
   soil-compaction episode, Sprout doesn't just find compacted dirt —
   she realizes it's compacted because it's *her own* daily path to
   the swing. The mystery becomes personal, not just factual.)
3. **A felt, human parallel, not just an explanation.** Somewhere in
   the episode, the science should translate into something the
   character (usually Sprout) has *felt in her own body or heart* —
   being hugged too tight, being squeezed on a crowded bench, holding
   your breath. This is literally "seeing with the heart": the
   invisible mechanism (root oxygen, soil compaction, whatever it is)
   becomes visible through empathy, not diagrams.
4. **A closing line with double meaning.** The tagline should work
   literally (the gardening/nature fact) *and* metaphorically (a life
   lesson about attention, patience, or love) at the same time.
   Compare a flat instruction ("Check the soil before you water") to
   a line that means two things at once ("Love needs room to
   breathe"). Always prefer the second kind.

**Produced examples of taglines that pass this test:**
- *"Look closer before you decide."* (winter plant damage — also
  about not judging by appearances)
- *"Give the ground room to breathe."* (soil compaction — also about
  over-visiting the things/places we love)
- *"Love needs room to breathe."* (overwatering — explicitly about
  love expressed as excess, not just plant care)

When brainstorming, generate the tagline *early* and test whether it
means something even to someone who has never gardened. If it
doesn't, the idea needs another pass.

## The Episode Formula

A reliable 8-beat discovery arc (originally built for an 8-image
social carousel, but works as a general story structure regardless of
final format):

1. **Establish** — the setting, quietly. No characters need to be
   emphasized yet.
2. **Wonder** — Sprout notices something good/hopeful/interesting
   nearby (often used as contrast for what's coming).
3. **Mystery / concern** — she notices the actual anomaly. A genuine
   question, not alarm.
4. **Investigate setup** — Brooks arrives, gets to her eye level, and
   redirects from assumption to evidence ("What do you notice?"). He
   never answers directly here.
5. **Investigate / focus** — close, tactile, sensory. Sprout does the
   testing herself.
6. **Discovery** — Sprout finds the key evidence. This is *her*
   moment, not Brooks'. Ideally this is also where the personal-stakes
   turn (see Little Prince Lens, point 2) lands.
7. **Understand** — Brooks explains, but briefly, and Sprout offers
   her own felt/human analogy partway through (see point 3). Brooks
   confirms rather than lectures.
8. **Care / close** — a concrete, cautious action, proposed by Sprout
   where possible, confirmed by Brooks. Ends on the double-meaning
   tagline.

**Discovery ownership rule:** Sprout notices, questions, investigates,
and discovers. Brooks guides, redirects, and interprets *after* the
fact. He never supplies the answer before she's found evidence for it.

**Dialogue economy:** keep it light — most beats carry one short line,
a few carry two or three at most. If a line could be shown instead of
said, show it. If Brooks' line reads like a lecture, cut it down to a
question.

## The Discovery Badge System

Badges celebrate curiosity and relationships built with nature — never
rank, speed, or competition. They accumulate; they're never removed
or redesigned casually. Organized into **Nature Families**, each with
several named badges. When an episode idea needs a badge, **pick an
existing one that fits** rather than inventing a new name — only flag
a new badge if nothing existing fits, and mark it clearly as
*proposed, not official.*

**Nature Families:** Plants, Trees, Flowers, Fungi, Grasses,
Pollinators, Insects, Birds, Butterflies, Mammals, Rocks, Soil, Water,
Weather, Winter, Night, Sunlight, Seeds, Leaves, Moss, Hidden Life.

**Examples already in use or available** (not exhaustive):
Soil Detective, Earthworm Friend, Compost Explorer, Moisture Tracker,
Seed Starter, Leaf Detective, Plant Friend, Wildflower Watcher,
Morning Listener, Nest Observer, Feather Finder, Bee Friend, Butterfly
Watcher, Cloud Watcher, Rain Observer, Snow Explorer, Pond Explorer,
Creek Guardian, Tree Friend, Bark Detective, Moss Explorer, Habitat
Helper, Pollinator Garden Builder.

Some badges are earned through story arcs rather than nature topics
(e.g. a badge about a milestone in the *relationship*, not a fact) —
these exist too, but are rarer and more significant.

## Quick Creative Tests

Run every idea through these before pitching it:

- **The Brooks Test** — would Brooks actually say this, or does he
  sound like a lecturer? If the latter, turn the line into a question.
- **The Sprout Test** — would a real 9-year-old notice/say this, or
  does she sound like a small adult? If the latter, simplify.
- **The Walk Outside Test** — after this story, would someone
  genuinely want to go look at a tree, the grass, the clouds? If not,
  the idea hasn't earned its ending yet.
- **The "Could This Happen Tomorrow?" Test** — could a child
  plausibly experience this in their own backyard tomorrow? If not,
  it's too exotic for this franchise.
- **The Studio Rule** — whenever choosing between more action or more
  curiosity, always choose curiosity.

## Hard Guardrails (do not cross these when brainstorming)

- Always **Ranger Brooks** (named) and **Sprout** — never a generic or
  unnamed mascot.
- Sprout is **always 9 years old** — never a range, never a younger
  "stage" version, unless explicitly writing a flagged origin/flashback
  story (and even then, her design doesn't change, only her missing
  loupe/Discovery Band as a one-time device).
- No badge-progression/ring/stage system — badges are discrete, named,
  and accumulate; there is no meter or level.
- No invented science. Distinguish observation from hypothesis; when
  uncertain, characters may say "I don't know yet."
- No villains, no danger-driven plots, no fear as motivation.
- No lecturing — if a scene is only "teaching facts," it needs a
  discovery moment instead.
- Every idea is a **proposal**, not canon, until the project owner
  approves it.

## How to Brainstorm With This Document

A good prompt to pair with this file, when talking to an AI:

> "Using the attached Lawn With Care Universe Story Bible, propose
> [N] new episode ideas. For each one, give me: (1) the small, ordinary
> observation that starts it, (2) the central mystery question, (3)
> the real science or nature fact involved, (4) the personal-stakes
> twist that implicates Sprout directly, (5) the felt/human analogy
> that makes the invisible mechanism visible, (6) a closing tagline
> with double meaning, and (7) which existing Discovery Badge fits (or
> flag if none does). Run each idea through the Brooks Test, the
> Sprout Test, and the Walk Outside Test before including it."

See `12-Brainstorm-Kickoff-Prompt.md` for a ready-to-paste version of
this.
